Settling in Bulgaria — Papers and Property, in One Place

If you're moving your life to Bulgaria — buying, already own, or are still deciding where — there's a fixed sequence of things that actually need doing, regardless of which town you end up in. Most of it isn't optional and most of it has a deadline.

Talk through your sequence hello@banskoconcierge.com

We work across Bulgaria. The examples on this page are drawn from Bansko and the surrounding region because that's where we have the deepest first-hand experience — the steps themselves are the same wherever you settle.

One thing sets our position apart, and it belongs here, not at the bottom of the page: we're a concierge office and a construction/renovation company under one roof. Most people sort out their papers with one company and their property with another, and the two never talk to each other. Here, the person who registered your address and the person who inspects your roof work from the same file.

Step 1

Residency

If you're arriving from the United Kingdom: since Brexit, UK nationals are third-country nationals in Bulgaria. For any stay beyond 90 days you need a Type D long-stay visa, applied for at the Bulgarian embassy in London or the consulate in Edinburgh before you travel.

Once you arrive, the clock starts: within 14 days you must present yourself in person at the Migration Directorate to apply for a residence permit, issued for 12 months initially and renewed annually.

The part that catches most people out isn't the paperwork at the office — it's the landlord. To register your address, which the permit requires, a signed lease is not enough. Your landlord has to appear in person before a Bulgarian notary, with their ID and the original title deed, and sign a declaration consenting to your registration at that address. A declaration signed at home does not count.

  • Not every landlord is willing to make that trip, and it isn't something a solicitor in London can arrange for you.
  • There is also a limit on how many people can be registered at one address; if a property is already close to that limit, the municipality can refuse your registration even with a willing landlord.

If this is relevant to you, we can arrange it for you: we contact your landlord, explain what's required, organise the notary appointment, and confirm beforehand that they have their ID, the original title deed and the lease with them. This covers the landlord/notary step specifically — we don't file the residence application itself and we're not your legal adviser. Pricing depends on location and is quoted on request; best to raise it before you sign a lease, not after.

For nationals of other countries, the residency route differs by nationality and purpose (EU/EEA freedom of movement, non-EU long-stay visa categories, investor/property routes). We're glad to point you to the right starting question for your situation, even where we can't advise on it directly.

Step 2

Your vehicle

If you're bringing a car from outside the EU, there's a fixed path: customs clearance, an individual type-approval (ИО), then registration — in that order, and not interchangeable.

The individual type-approval costs €51.13 and is valid for 10 years, without expiry. First registration itself is €17.90. On top of that comes an environmental levy (ПУДООС) of roughly €196.85.

The bigger number is customs: duty of 10% plus 20% VAT, together around 32% of the customs value — unless your move qualifies as a change-of-residence exemption under EU Regulation 1186/2009, which removes it. Whether you qualify is the single biggest cost variable in this process, and it's worth establishing before you ship the car, not after.

Three taxes that do not apply, worth knowing because people often budget for them anyway: no excise duty (abolished 2010), no registration tax, and no acquisition tax under the Local Taxes Act for a first-registration import.

Because the cost genuinely splits into two different outcomes — roughly €500 with the exemption, roughly €1,890 without — we don't quote a flat price for this.

What we offer is a fixed-fee assessment: €290 — a written answer on where your specific vehicle and situation land before you commit to shipping anything.

Step 3

Buying or renting

Whether you're buying or renting, the practical risk is the same: you're assessing a property you can't easily revisit before deciding, in a market with less standardised disclosure than you may be used to. Step 4

Checking the property's condition

Before you buy — or before you finish moving in — the condition of the building matters more than the finish. A pre-purchase inspection isn't an opinion; it's a checked list, each item answered, so the one thing that matters isn't the one nobody thought to look at. This is where the construction side of our work starts, and it's the same team that will be doing the work if the inspection finds something.

Putting it right
